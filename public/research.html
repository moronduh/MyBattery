Research

Reframing Productivity
for Mental Health

Design Principles for Wellbeing-Centered Task Management

Miranda Johnson Capstone — Final Draft
Most productivity tools assume we all have steady motivation, predictable energy, and a linear workflow. For people dealing with depression or fluctuating mental health, those assumptions break down quickly. This paper proposes six design principles for productivity apps that prioritize sustainable engagement and wellbeing over raw output — tested through MyBattery, a prototype mobile app.

1 — Background

The problem with
productivity as usual

Depression affects nearly 18.3% of adults (Witters, 2025), bringing significant disruptions to cognitive control, motivation, and emotional regulation. Despite this reality, everyday digital tools still expect users to have perfectly stable motivation and make steady, linear progress.

A lot of HCI research connects productivity wins straight to interface usability and system efficiency — but it has a massive blind spot: it treats users as always mentally stable, motivated, and ready to adapt. Shifting mental health is completely ignored as a core design factor.

Interestingly, people managing depression rarely rely on just one system. Instead, they piece together adaptable, multi-tool setups — picking up and dropping different apps depending on their immediate energy, attention span, and mood. Yet these lessons from digital health research haven't broken into mainstream productivity design. Everyday apps keep prioritizing speed and output over psychological safety, frequently failing the very users most vulnerable to guilt, shame, and self-doubt.

2 — Research Gap

Ten gaps in existing
research and design

A literature review and comparative app analysis surfaced ten recurring gaps between how tools are built and how people with depression actually use them.

Depression & motivation
Limited guidance exists on translating research on depression and motivation into actionable design decisions for everyday productivity tools.
Hidden assumptions
Productivity systems often assume constant motivation, stable energy, and linear progress — but these assumptions are rarely examined critically.
Efficiency over wellbeing
Few productivity models prioritize mental wellbeing, sustainability, or psychological safety over output maximization.
HCI blind spots
Existing HCI work emphasizes usability and efficiency while leaving fluctuating mental health, motivation, and emotional states largely unaddressed.
Mental health variability
Variability in mental health is rarely treated as a central design consideration in productivity tools.
Sustaining engagement
There is limited guidance on sustaining engagement with digital tools during periods of low motivation or energy.
Multi-tool ecosystems
Research on how users combine and switch between multiple productivity tools is limited, despite its relevance to real-world use.
Guilt & shame
Little research examines how productivity interfaces may contribute to guilt, shame, or negative self-evaluation in vulnerable users.
Ethical design
Clear frameworks connecting productivity systems with wellbeing, autonomy, and mental health ethics are lacking.
Bridging disciplines
A disconnect remains between findings in digital mental health research and their application to non-clinical productivity tools.
3 — Methodology

A qualitative,
design-focused approach

This research uses a qualitative, design-focused approach rooted in HCI and human-centered design principles. Rather than measuring efficiency, the goal is to build a framework for productivity tools that actually works for people dealing with depression.

The methodology included an academic literature review drawing from HCI, human factors engineering, clinical psychology, and digital health — paired with a hands-on comparative analysis of widely used mental wellness and productivity apps on iOS.

The app comparison made the core tension visible: mental wellness apps and productivity apps are building for completely different assumptions about who the user is.
Wellness apps

Wysa, Daylio, Sanvello, Youper, Happify, MoodKit

  • Mood tracking
  • CBT tools
  • Reflective journaling
  • Supportive prompts
  • AI chat (some)
  • Community (some)
Productivity apps

Todoist, Things 3, Notion, TickTick, Habitica

  • Task management
  • Habits & streaks
  • Reminders
  • Progress metrics
  • Collaboration
  • Consistency-driven feedback
The disconnect is obvious. Mental wellness apps routinely include mood tracking, reflective journaling, and supportive prompts. Productivity apps center task completion, progress metrics, and consistency-driven feedback. These two categories have almost nothing in common in terms of how they handle the user's internal state — yet mental health directly affects the cognitive functioning required to be productive.
4 — Design Principles

Six principles for
mental health-aware productivity

Each principle targets a specific embedded assumption in current tools and replaces it with a concrete design requirement. Together they form a framework that moves from how tools handle energy and effort, to language and tone, to cognitive load and user autonomy.

P1

Expect and Accommodate Lower Energy

Most productivity tools assume a flat, reliable baseline of human energy. When users skip a day, slow down, or fall short of targets, the interface treats it as a failure. For someone with depression, this is not an edge case — it is a recurring reality. Tools need to let users signal their current capacity and automatically adjust daily targets without making them feel bad.

Design rule Build interfaces that flex based on user energy inputs rather than enforcing a standard workload every day. Low energy states, including hibernate, should be treated as normal interaction modes, not exceptions.
P2

Value Sustainable Effort Over Pure Speed

Traditional productivity tools focus on speed and maximum output. That performance-first framing ignores psychological safety and fails users for whom slow, consistent effort is the realistic goal. Interfaces should surface longitudinal trends and small wins rather than fixating on daily completion rates.

Design rule Design dashboards that highlight consistency and incremental progress. Remove aggressive countdowns, high-stakes completion percentages, and streak-based pressure. Progress should be measured relative to the user's capacity, not against a fixed standard.
P3

Use Dignity-Preserving, Non-Clinical Language

The language an interface uses carries implicit assumptions about who the user is and what they should be capable of. Clinical framing, features that feel like surveillance, and tonally mismatched prompts can cause users to disengage or stop being honest with the tool. This principle emerged directly from formative evaluation feedback — one participant noted that distress keyword detection made her feel monitored rather than supported, and said she would not share how she was really feeling if she thought the app was watching for it.

Design rule Review all interface copy for clinical tone, unintended humor, and surveillance cues. Use plain, warm language that treats the full range of energy states as normal. Make supportive resources — including breathing exercises, grounding tools, and crisis links — persistently accessible, not conditionally surfaced.
P4

Remove Punitive UI Patterns

Daily streaks, red overdue alerts, and aggressive reminder notifications are standard in most productivity tools. For users who already struggle with guilt or negative self-evaluation, these patterns can trigger shame that leads to abandoning the app entirely. Non-punitive design reframes the interface as a collaborator rather than an accountability enforcer.

Design rule Remove streaks, penalties, and urgency-coded visual alerts. Present data in a neutral, objective way. Incomplete tasks should carry forward without accumulating negative visual weight.
P5

Simplify Complex Workflows Automatically

Depression impairs cognitive control, which makes decision-making and task initiation significantly harder. When an app shows a large, unstructured task list, users in a low-energy episode often hit a wall and cannot start anything. The system should take some of that work off the user by breaking projects into small, manageable steps and reducing the number of things asking for attention at once.

Design rule Provide easy-to-trigger task decomposition options. Limit the number of visible tasks to match the user's declared energy state. Prioritize helping users start over helping them complete everything.
P6

Protect User Choice and Self-Trust

Digital health tools often fail by locking users into rigid pathways. Productivity apps do the same through inflexible calendars and interactions that are hard to exit or undo. Systems should respect autonomy: users need to be able to pause, shift, or restructure their day without friction. The formative evaluation identified several places where this principle was not yet implemented — including the absence of a cancel option during wind-down, and difficulty exiting the SOS modal.

Design rule Every automated suggestion should be easy to override, bypass, or dismiss. Any significant system state — such as wind-down, hibernate, or crisis resources — should have a clear, low-friction exit. Destructive actions like account deletion should require confirmatory steps.
5 — Interface Architecture

How the principles
live in MyBattery

The six principles were implemented in a working prototype accessible at mybatteryapp.com. The following describes how each design consideration became a concrete interaction layer.

Daily Check-in

Readiness Assessment

The app opens with a daily readiness check covering energetic categorization (High / Medium / Low / Hibernate), affective mapping, an optional brain dump, and a somatic reflection prompt. Each component is voluntary — the interface does not score or evaluate the inputs.

Task Architecture

Three-Tier Task Hierarchy

Tasks are organized into Must, Should, and Could tiers. The number of tasks visible in each tier scales automatically based on the user's declared energy state. Each task includes a duration estimate and a bi-directional energy impact value — whether completing it will drain or recharge the user. Incomplete tasks reset daily without accumulating negative visual weight.

Core Metaphor

The Energy Battery

MyBattery replaces conventional productivity metrics with a visual Energy Battery running 0–100%. It updates dynamically as the user completes tasks and events, and can be overridden manually at any time. When battery drops below ~20%, the app enters a reduced complexity mode: fewer tasks are surfaced and the interface simplifies.

Wellness Hub

Always-On Wellness Tools

The Wellness Hub offers grounding exercises, guided breathing (Box, 4-7-8, and custom patterns), a micro-journal, a Pomodoro timer, and self-administered screenings for depression (PHQ-9), anxiety (GAD-7), and burnout. Crisis resources are available through an always-accessible SOS modal. Sensitive data is not stored.

Wind-Down

Diurnal Closure Ritual

When the system detects a low-capacity state or the day ends, it prompts a wind-down sequence including activity summaries, gratitude prompts, a mood check-in, and a reflective sentence-building exercise. The intent is to give the day a soft close, normalizing rest and preventing the psychological weight of unfinished tasks from carrying into the evening.

Privacy

Data Sovereignty

Users can enable Privacy Mode (blurs task names and mood values on screen) and Gentle Mode (softens all system language). Task data is encrypted end-to-end using AES-GCM-256. These features give users meaningful control over their data and how the app presents information.

6 — Formative Evaluation

What five participants
actually found

A formative evaluation was conducted with five participants, including a licensed therapist with an MS in Mental Health Counseling and a participant with an MS in Experimental Psychology. The evaluation used an informal think-aloud format — participants explored the app on their own and shared verbal and written feedback as they went.

"When the mental health scaffolding is working well, it becomes invisible. The app just feels like a thoughtful productivity tool."

— Key finding: no participant flagged the mental health framing as unusual or clinical, suggesting the design principles were integrated effectively into the interaction layer rather than sitting on top of it.
P6 — User Control

Missing Exit Affordances

Wind-down had no cancel option. The SOS modal was hard to dismiss. Settings changes did not prompt users to save before leaving. These issues pointed to a real gap between Principle 6 as stated and how it was implemented.

P3 — Language

Tone and Surveillance Concerns

The energy label "High" following "How are you arriving today" read as accidentally funny. Distress keyword detection made one participant feel watched — she said she would not be honest with the app if she thought it was scanning her input.

Clinical Feedback

Wellness Scaffolding

The licensed therapist noted that grounding exercises (suited to hypoarousal) and breathing exercises (suited to hyperarousal) were presented as a flat list with no guidance on when to use each. A prompt-first model would better reflect clinical practice.

Clinical Feedback

Crisis Resources

988 was listed with text options only, but 988 supports both call and text. More broadly, treating crisis resources as the only mental health pathway sends the wrong message — many users who need support are not in crisis.

P1 + P5

Feature Gaps

Participants wanted repeatable tasks, configurable stillness break timing, a mascot for battery visualization, font size accessibility settings, and an energy drain value surfaced as a primary interaction rather than buried in an overflow menu.

Build Issues

Technical Debt

Navigation bar switching on swipe, tutorial referencing the old app name (Liveflo), breathing orb not holding its expanded state, and a stillness timer with potential seizure risk were flagged for future development — these are build-level, not principle-level, failures.

What worked well

The low-battery-triggered wind-down prompt was described as a natural and well-timed nudge.

The tri-tier task structure (Must / Should / Could) was praised as clear and easy to read.

The idea that tasks could carry energy drain or recharge values resonated strongly — participants immediately grasped and engaged with the concept.

Adding a mascot to make battery state more expressive was independently suggested by one participant — aligning with a feature already in scope.

Conclusion

Productivity isn't about
crushing a to-do list

For someone fighting depression, productivity is about staying engaged without losing your dignity, and building a relationship with your own capacity that is honest, flexible, and above all, kind. Mainstream productivity apps are built for an idealized user who doesn't actually exist. By combining academic literature, a hands-on app comparison, and a working prototype, this research developed six core principles that move toward a more humane definition of getting things done.
